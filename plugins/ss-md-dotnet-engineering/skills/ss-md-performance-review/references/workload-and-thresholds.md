## Workload, thresholds, and operational boundaries

Use for reported symptoms (slow requests, growing memory, hangs, timeouts, starvation, or failures under load), explicit budgets, or concrete implementation risks such as unbounded fan-out or contention over shared state. Ordinary use of `async`, a collection, or a parallel API is not by itself a reason to launch an optimization exercise.

- Establish representative input sizes, request or arrival rates, concurrent work, burst duration, downstream capacity, deployment limits, and relevant failure conditions. Inspect the affected execution path and shared resources; a locally faster operation can make other requests slower or unavailable.
- Identify the metric, threshold, scope, and conditions under which it must hold. For example, "p95 endpoint latency below 200 ms at 100 requests/second with the agreed payload mix" is more meaningful than "fast." Identify whether latency includes queueing and dependencies, and whether a memory budget means managed heap, total process memory, or a container limit.
- Use authoritative requirements and observations. If a missing threshold, workload, or consistency decision materially affects implementation or acceptance, ask rather than invent a number. For exploratory diagnosis without a target, report a baseline and opportunities without claiming a requirement is satisfied.
- Consider startup, steady-state, peak load, and sustained operation when relevant. Allocation rate is not retained memory; average latency is not tail latency; configured parallelism is not demonstrated capacity.
- Bound active work and queued work against CPU, memory, connection pools, and downstream limits. A per-request concurrency cap may still permit process-wide overload across many requests or replicas. More parallelism is not automatically more throughput.
- Define overload behavior deliberately: waiting, rejection, dropping, or delayed processing must match the contract. Preserve cancellation, timeouts, error reporting, ordering, and cleanup. Do not silently drop work, weaken consistency, increase timeouts, or add retries to disguise a capacity problem.
- Resource exhaustion, contention, thread-pool starvation, and overload-related availability belong in this investigation. Race conditions, deadlocks, and leaks require correctness and lifecycle reasoning, not just speed measurements.
- Availability is broader than performance. Failover, disaster recovery, deployment continuity, and availability objectives without an established resource or execution mechanism require reliability or infrastructure analysis beyond this guide. Identify that need rather than present a benchmark as evidence of availability.
- Hard real-time guarantees need suitable platform analysis and evidence; ordinary .NET benchmarks or a good percentile do not prove a worst-case deadline.
